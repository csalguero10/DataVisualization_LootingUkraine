Destruction, looting, and cultural appropriation in Ukraine across history and war

01
/ About

Is cultural loss in Ukraine only a consequence of war?

  • WHAT
  • WHY
  • HOW

Since the beginning of the Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the scale of damage to cultural heritage has reached alarming levels. According to official sources, more than 2,000 cultural objects have been looted, and over 500 cultural sites have been damaged or destroyed (data updated to December 2025).


Beyond these numbers, the destruction has affected sites of outstanding cultural value, including historic urban centers such as Odesa and Lviv, both inscribed on UNESCO’s World Heritage List. Throughout the conflict, art and cultural heritage have played a key role, not only as victims of violence but also as symbolic targets.

Cultural loss in Ukraine is often framed as a direct consequence of the ongoing war. However, a closer look at institutional data suggests a more complex picture.


While large-scale destruction of cultural sites intensifies with the escalation of military violence in 2022, the appropriation and relocation of Ukrainian cultural objects appears to predate the full-scale invasion. This raises questions about whether cultural harm should be interpreted only as collateral damage, or as part of longer-term processes linked to power, identity, and historical narratives.

This project explores cultural harm in Ukraine not only as an effect of armed conflict, but as a longer-term process that includes both destruction and cultural appropriation, developing across different temporal and political contexts.


Through the analysis of institutional datasets, we examine destruction and looting as distinct yet interconnected forms of cultural harm. By connecting data on damaged sites, looted objects, conflict events, and museum collections, the project offers an evidence-based starting point for a broader discussion on cultural heritage, conflict, and power.

The Congress underlines that the targeting and looting of cultural sites appear to reflect a systematic policy aiming at erasing Ukraine’s historical and cultural identity The destruction of cultural heritage in Ukraine, point 5 Council of Europe, Congress of Local and Regional Authorities (2024)
02
/ Research Focus

The destruction of cultural heritage during armed conflicts is not a new phenomenon. However, the Ukrainian case raises a crucial question:

Are these acts the consequence of warfare, or do they reflect a more structured strategy of cultural domination?

This question becomes particularly relevant when practices of cultural appropriation and relocation are observed not only during active hostilities, but also in periods preceding large-scale military escalation.

The ongoing war is highly complex, and so is the phenomenon of Russification, a historical term used to describe processes of cultural assimilation in which non-Russian populations are encouraged or compelled to adopt Russian language, culture, and identity. Historians have used this concept to analyze policies implemented during the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, often in connection with political centralization rather than cultural exchange. Historically, Russification has affected multiple dimensions of cultural life, including language, education, architecture, religion, artistic production, and the symbolic transformation of public space. Scholars commonly distinguish between the spread of Russian language or culture (Russianization) and deeper processes of identity transformation and assimilation (Russification).

In contemporary contexts, these dynamics re-emerge explicitly. Russian state narratives have repeatedly denied Ukraine's cultural and historical autonomy, describing Russians, Ukrainians and Belarusians as "one people" and Ukrainian statehood as artificial (Putin, 2021). Concerns about the nature of cultural damage have also been raised at the international level: UN Special Rapporteurs (2023) warned that the destruction hinders access to cultural heritage, and the Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities (2024) stated that the targeting and looting of cultural sites "appear to reflect a systematic policy".

Attacks against cultural property are prohibited under international law, notably the 1954 Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict, to which both Ukraine and Russia are parties. At the same time, cultural and political dynamics are rarely unidirectional or unambiguous, and patterns in data cannot by themselves establish intent. For this reason, this project does not frame the phenomenon in binary terms, but investigates what patterns in data can, and cannot, reveal.

03
/ Datasets

The project integrates multiple institutional datasets that document different dimensions of cultural harm in Ukraine. Rather than relying on a single source, we combined data on looted objects, damaged cultural sites, artists, museum collections, conflict events, and heritage protection lists. Each dataset captures a specific aspect of the phenomenon. Together, they allow us to analyze how destruction, looting, cultural appropriation, and violence interact across time, space, and institutional contexts.

01.   Ukrainian War Sanctions Registry

This dataset documents cases of cultural property expropriation recorded in the official Ukrainian War Sanctions Registry. It records 2,287 stolen objects (paintings, archaeological artefacts, coins, icons, decorative arts) with category, dating, place and year of incident and a description of the circumstances. It is maintained by Ukraine's Main Directorate of Intelligence, a party to the conflict, and is used here as a record of documented cases.

02.   UNESCO Destroyed and Damaged Sites

This dataset is based on UNESCO's official list of cultural sites damaged or destroyed since February 2022. The version used here (annotated by Linked4Resilience) contains 351 sites with damage first reported between 2022 and 2024: museums, religious buildings, monuments and historic buildings. UNESCO's count has grown since this snapshot.

03.   Linked 4 Resilience

The Linked4Resilience knowledge graph is built on UNESCO's list of damaged sites. Volunteers annotated each entry with addresses, Wikipedia pages and news reports to identify and geolocate it; 211 of the 351 sites are retained as unambiguously identified and reliably geolocated, and were reconciled with Wikidata for spatial analysis.

04.  UNESCO Protection Lists

The 8 Ukrainian World Heritage properties and the 16 sites on the Tentative List (candidates, not inscribed). They are used to measure how close recorded damage came to internationally recognised heritage.

05.  ACLED Conflict Events Dataset

The Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) provides georeferenced records of political violence in Ukraine: 199,813 events from 1 January 2018 (start of ACLED's coverage of Ukraine) to 21 December 2024, including battles, explosions and remote violence, violence against civilians and strategic developments. It is used to describe the conflict context around damaged sites and looting locations.

06.   Hermitage Museum Collection Data

This dataset comes from an open data initiative by Texty.org.ua that archived collection inventories of Russian museums, including the Hermitage, before they were removed from the museums' websites. From 33,391 Hermitage records we extracted 14,508 objects of Ukrainian provenance, with acquisition years from 1848 to 2015.

07.  ICOM Red Lists

The ICOM Red List of Ukraine is an emergency reference tool developed by the International Council of Museums. It identifies categories of Ukrainian cultural objects that are particularly vulnerable to theft, illicit trafficking, and looting during armed conflict. Rather than documenting individual incidents, the Red List provides typological guidance to support prevention, monitoring, and protection efforts, and was used in this project as a contextual framework for interpreting patterns of cultural risk.

08.  Wikidata Ukrainian Artists Database

Metadata on Ukrainian artists (birthplaces, life dates, movements, institutions) retrieved through SPARQL queries to Wikidata. It was collected to document the artists represented among stolen works and is not used in the analyses presented on this page.

04
/ Historical Context

Throughout its history, Ukraine has been shaped by continuous political instability and long periods of foreign domination. From the medieval state of Kievan Rus’, centered in Kyiv between the ninth and eleventh centuries, the region played a key political and cultural role in Eastern Europe. However, internal divisions and the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century led to the collapse of this early state. After that, Ukrainian territories were gradually controlled by different powers, including the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Crimean Khanate, the Ottoman Empire, the Habsburg monarchy, the Russian Empire, and later the Soviet Union. These constant changes in political control greatly delayed Ukraine’s path to independence and weakened the development of stable cultural institutions.

This complex historical background had a direct impact on Ukrainian cultural heritage. Cultural life was largely managed from imperial centers, while local institutions were limited or dismantled. Although there were moments of partial autonomy, such as the Cossack Hetmanate in the seventeenth century, these periods were short-lived and eventually suppressed, especially after Ukraine’s incorporation into the Russian Empire in the late eighteenth century. Policies restricting the Ukrainian language and identity, together with the centralization of archives, artworks, and religious objects in cities like Moscow and St. Petersburg, made cultural dispossession, in the view of many historians, a long-term process rather than an isolated consequence of war.

The twentieth century further intensified these dynamics. After brief attempts to establish an independent state following the First World War, most Ukrainian territory became part of the Soviet Union. During this period, political repression, forced famine, and centralized cultural policies caused severe damage to Ukrainian heritage. The Second World War led to particularly heavy losses, including hundreds of thousands of museum objects, millions of books, and thousands of religious artifacts. Although international agreements, such as the 1954 Hague Convention, were created to protect cultural property during armed conflict, their enforcement in the Ukrainian case remained weak and often influenced by political interests.

Ukraine achieved internationally recognized independence only in 1991, much later than many other European countries. This late independence helps explain why the protection and restitution of Ukrainian cultural heritage remain unresolved issues today. Despite the existence of international treaties designed to prevent cultural looting and destruction, meaningful restitution has been limited. The ongoing conflict, intensified by the full-scale Russian invasion of 2022, shows that cultural heritage is not only a historical concern but a central element of contemporary geopolitical struggles. Understanding Ukraine’s long and complex history is therefore essential to grasp both the scale of its cultural losses and the challenges involved in addressing them.

05
/ Methodology

This project does not aim to take a political position. Instead, it adopts a data-driven and exploratory approach, focusing on the documentation and analysis of verified sources. We integrated and cross-referenced multiple datasets related to: damaged and destroyed cultural sites, looted cultural objects, conflict and invasion events, museum collections and artist databases.

A central part of our work consisted in monitoring, cleaning, reconciling, and aligning institutional data produced by organizations with different mandates, update rhythms, and documentation standards.

By putting these heterogeneous sources into dialogue, the project proposes a methodological contribution to the study of cultural heritage destruction in contemporary conflicts. More broadly, it highlights the importance of institutional data monitoring and of cross-dataset integration as tools to communicate and investigate complex phenomena such as war, cultural loss, and imperial dynamics.

Data, code and the full analysis are openly available: see the project notebook and the GitHub repository.

/ Research questions

To translate these concerns into a data-driven investigation, we formulated research questions focused on observable patterns in cultural destruction and looting. Using institutional datasets, we examine when, where, and how different forms of cultural harm occur, without assuming intent or causality.

The project develops four interconnected investigations. Select one of the four research questions to open its analysis.

Research question 1
Historical displacement: Ukrainian heritage in the Hermitage collection

When, where and under which conditions did Ukrainian material culture enter Russian institutional collections?

We analyse 14,508 objects of Ukrainian provenance recorded in archived inventories of the State Hermitage Museum in St Petersburg (43.5% of the 33,391 records archived by Texty.org.ua). Rather than retrospectively classifying every historical transfer as illegal looting, this investigation looks at provenance, institutional centralisation and changing legal frameworks across the Russian Empire, the Soviet period and the post-Soviet era. It provides the long-term background against which contemporary appropriation (RQ2) can be read.

Sub-questions
  • 1. When did the objects enter the museum?
  • 2. Where in Ukraine do they come from?
  • 3. What kinds of material culture are they?

According to historian Serhiy Kot, during the Second World War alone Ukraine lost up to 250,000 museum items and 50 million books, and items taken from Ukraine accounted for 55% of all cultural objects lost by the Soviet Union during the war. Restitution between Ukraine and Russia remains particularly complex, also because Russian official discourse denies Ukraine's distinct historical existence.

The data: 14,508 Ukrainian objects among 33,391 Hermitage records

Data cleaning involved translating Cyrillic text into English, fixing longitude and latitude values that were swapped in the original data, normalising more than 50 date formats (from millennia to single years), matching coordinates to current Ukrainian oblasts and grouping materials into broader categories. Each object was assigned to a historical period of creation.

The map shows where the objects were found, by period of creation.

When: phases of acquisition

Acquisition years are recorded for 10,667 objects (1848–2015). 9,271 of them, 87% of the dated objects and 64% of the whole dataset, entered the museum during the Soviet period (1922–1991), against 607 under the Russian Empire and 789 after 1991. No acquisitions are recorded for 1917–1921.

Under the Russian Empire, centralist policies directed valuable finds to the imperial capital: when Kyiv intellectuals proposed a local museum in 1888, the Governor-General rejected it as "unnecessary". From 1859 the Imperial Archaeological Commission coordinated excavations across the empire and sent the finest finds to the Hermitage, reflecting a policy of centralising the most valuable material.

The Soviet era in the literature. According to Serhiy Kot, between 1918 and 1923 the Bolsheviks seized at least 10 million objects worth 10 billion rubles (in 1913 prices); in Kyiv alone, 150 private collections containing 200,000 objects were seized in 1922. Historians describe several mechanisms through which Ukrainian heritage was transferred to Russian institutions, often justified with protective, administrative or ideological arguments:

  • Export for sale: in the 1920s and 1930s, artefacts were sold abroad to raise state revenue.
  • "Union-wide" finds: valuable archaeological finds were deemed of "union-wide significance" and sent to central museums.
  • Exhibitions: items sent to Moscow for exhibitions were sometimes never returned.
  • Museum seizures and religious confiscations.

The dilemma of centralisation. The transfer of artefacts to St Petersburg and Moscow protected some of them from theft or dispersal into private collections. At the same time, Ukrainian initiatives to establish local museums and conduct research were obstructed: state funding to acquire valuable finds from local excavators was granted to Russian museums. Ukrainian scholars, museum professionals and philanthropists often acted as intermediaries. The philanthropist Bohdan Khanenko, for example, tried to convince the Hermitage to buy a gold plaque with an emerald found in Poltava province; his efforts failed and the object was lost.

After 1991. Acquisitions did not stop with Ukrainian independence: 789 objects entered the collection after 1991, mostly from Crimean sites (369 from the ancient settlement and necropolis of Nymphaeum, 124 from Solkhat) and 32 from Volyn province (2004). These cases raise questions about the legal frameworks governing excavations and export permits: whether the objects were acquired through authorised excavations with proper documentation, or through illicit channels. Some may have been legally documented at the time; the provenance of others remains unclear, and current rules restrict the trade and export of cultural property of uncertain origin.

Where: oblasts of origin

Matched to current oblasts, 43% of the objects come from Crimea (including Sevastopol), 24% from Vinnytsia and 14% from Chernivtsi oblast. The find location with the most objects is Nemyriv in Vinnytsia oblast (3,203 objects). The concentrations correspond to sites of ancient cultures, in particular Scythian, Sarmatian and Trypillia sites.

Because the inventory uses historical place names (e.g. Solkhat, the medieval name of Staryi Krym), all coordinates were matched to current administrative boundaries.

What: material culture

Ceramics are the largest material category (6,670 objects, 46%), followed by stone (3,520, 24%) and metal (3,335, 23%). Most objects are everyday archaeological finds such as pottery and tools, but the collection also includes gold and precious objects, notably Scythian–Sarmatian goldwork, among the museum's best-known holdings.

Ukrainian scholars argue that these collections have also been used to support a historical narrative linking modern Russia to the ancient cultures of the Ukrainian steppe. The data show the concentration of such material in the museum, not how it is used.

Legal and ethical implications

After the dissolution of the USSR, Ukraine has argued that cultural property originating from its territory should be returned, while Russia treats objects transferred under Soviet rule as property of the Soviet state inherited by the Russian Federation. Because many transfers were legal under the rules of the time, restitution claims are difficult to pursue.

Russian official narratives describing Russians and Ukrainians as "one people" (Putin, 2021) frame this heritage as shared, which further complicates Ukrainian claims. The current war, with new losses in Mariupol and Kherson (RQ2), has renewed these questions.

Findings
  1. Scale. 14,508 objects of Ukrainian provenance, 43.5% of the archived Hermitage records.
  2. Acquisition. 87% of the objects with a known acquisition year entered the museum in the Soviet period; acquisitions continued after 1991 (789 objects, mostly from Crimean sites).
  3. Geography. Crimea (43%), Vinnytsia (24%) and Chernivtsi (14%) oblasts.
  4. Composition. Mostly archaeological finds: ceramics (46%), stone (24%) and metal (23%), including high-value goldwork.
  5. Interpretation. The data document a long-term, centralised flow of Ukrainian archaeological material to a central museum of the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union. Many transfers were legal at the time; provenance, not legality alone, is the key issue.

Research question 2
Contemporary looting: cultural objects stolen since 2014

What has been recorded as stolen since 2014, from where and when?

We analyse 2,287 cultural objects recorded as stolen in the War & Sanctions registry maintained by Ukraine's Main Directorate of Intelligence. The registry is produced by a party to the conflict and records the cases documented by Ukrainian institutions: we treat it as a record of documented cases and focus on what was taken, from where and when, without inferring motives from object categories alone.

Sub-questions
  • 1. What categories of objects are most represented?
  • 2. From where were they taken?
  • 3. When were they recorded as stolen?
  • 4. Which historical periods do they belong to?

On 8 April 2025, Ukraine's Minister of Culture and Strategic Communications, Mykola Tochytskyi, stated in an interview with Ukrinform that more than 1.7 million items of Ukrainian cultural heritage had been stolen by Russia since the beginning of the full-scale invasion. The registry analysed here documents 2,287 individual objects, a small part of the losses reported by Ukrainian authorities.

What is taken

Paintings are the largest category (874 objects), followed by ceramic products (450), graphic works (372) and metal products (150). The concentration is strong: 828 of the 874 paintings (95%) and all 372 graphic works come from a single institution, the Kherson Regional Art Museum. The data therefore cannot show a general preference for paintings: they show the removal of one museum's collection.

According to the registry, metal products include Scythian gold jewellery from the fourth century BC and ancient coins, mostly from archaeological sites in Crimea.

The Kherson collection includes Ukrainian modernist art. Kyiv was a centre of early twentieth-century modernism (Cubo-Futurism, Boichukism, Suprematism, Ukrainian Futurism), with artists such as Alexandra Exter, Oleksandr Bogomazov, Mykhailo Boichuk, Kazimir Malevich and Mykhail Semenko. Many were later repressed, executed or exiled under Stalin, in what is known as the "Executed Renaissance", and some are still labelled internationally as "Russian".

Where they come from

All objects come from 42 places in four regions: Crimea (906 objects from 37 archaeological sites and reserves), Kherson (1,300 objects from three museums and galleries), Donetsk (44 objects from the Kuindzhi Art Museum in Mariupol) and Zaporizhzhia (37 objects from the Stone Tomb reserve near Melitopol).

Since the annexation of Crimea in 2014, which Ukraine and most UN member states do not recognise (UN General Assembly Resolution 68/262), the registry records many Crimean finds as "appropriated during illegal excavations". Apart from 29 works from the Kuindzhi Art Museum recorded in 2014, all incidents are recorded in territories under Russian occupation.

When they were taken

The registry records thefts from 2014, not only after 2022: 507 objects are recorded between 2014 and 2021, 478 of them from archaeological sites in Crimea and 29 from the Kuindzhi Art Museum in Mariupol, recorded in 2014 under an exhibition agreement with Russian institutions. The 136 objects recorded in 2017 all come from Crimean archaeological sites; the registry does not explain this peak.

The largest annual figure, 1,263 objects in 2022, corresponds mostly to a single event: the removal of 1,184 objects from the Kherson Art Museum in autumn 2022, during the Russian withdrawal from the city. Excluding that museum, the yearly number of recorded thefts stays in the same range before and after 2022 (RQ3).

Which historical periods

The most represented periods are the Soviet period (868 objects), the Scythian–Sarmatian era (442), the Greek and Roman period (300) and the Russian Empire (288). Almost all Soviet-period objects (99%) come from the Kherson Art Museum; Scythian–Sarmatian and Greek–Roman objects come mainly from Crimean archaeological sites and from the Kherson Local History Museum.

Compared with the archaeological focus of earlier transfers (RQ1), the 2022 losses include a large share of modern art. Excluding the Kherson Art Museum, however, archaeological material still accounts for most recorded thefts (84%, RQ4), so the data do not show a general shift away from archaeology. Media reported in 2022 the removal of Scythian gold from the Melitopol Museum of Local History; those objects are not part of the registry data.

Documented cases

According to reports by Forbes and NBC News, works removed from the Kherson Art Museum in autumn 2022 were taken by truck to museums in occupied Crimea; works by Ivan Pokhytonov and Heorhii Kurnakov later surfaced there. The director of the Central Museum of Taurida in Simferopol, Andriy Malgin, told Radio Free Europe that the works were stored there "to protect" them: "These are not our paintings. We understand this very well [...] We just store them."

Similar transfers had been reported after 2014. According to Ukrainian prosecutors, dozens of paintings were removed from museum collections in Crimea, and 52 works, including Ivan Aivazovsky's Moonlit Night, Ivan Shishkin's Forest Road and Isaac Levitan's The Swamp, were added to Interpol's list of stolen works of art. In February 2024 Moonlit Night reappeared at a Moscow auction house and was sold for nearly one million dollars (Rubryka, 2024).

Legal and narrative framing

In December 2023 Russia adopted a law on the "integrity and indivisibility" of museum collections (TASS, 2023), which restricts the removal of objects from state museum collections. Critics argue that it could make the return of objects transferred from occupied territories more difficult. Russian officials describe such transfers as evacuation or protection, while Ukrainian authorities describe them as theft.

Several institutions and scholars interpret these losses as deliberate. Katharyn Hanson, research chief at the Smithsonian Cultural Rescue Initiative, stated that the looting was not "random or spontaneous acts by Russian soldiers" and that "our research suggests that this looting is state-sponsored by Russia". Interpol and ICOM have issued emergency tools to help identify stolen Ukrainian objects. This project reports these interpretations but does not test intent: the data show what was taken, from where and when.

Findings
  1. Concentration. 2,287 objects from 42 places; 1,233 from a single institution, the Kherson Regional Art Museum.
  2. What. Paintings are the largest category (874), but 95% of them come from the Kherson museum; excluding it, archaeological material accounts for most recorded thefts.
  3. Where. Four regions; apart from 29 works recorded in 2014 in Mariupol, all incidents are recorded in territories under Russian occupation.
  4. When. 507 objects between 2014 and 2021, almost all from Crimea; the 2022 peak corresponds to the removal of the Kherson museum collection.
  5. Limits. The registry records documented cases only; the year of incident is missing for 417 objects and coordinates refer to institutions, not to individual objects.

Research question 3
Destruction vs. looting: patterns of cultural harm

Do the destruction of cultural sites and the looting of cultural objects follow the same patterns in time, space and context of violence?

We compare 351 UNESCO-verified damaged cultural sites (damage first reported 2022–2024) with the 2,287 stolen objects of the War & Sanctions registry, and place both in the context of 199,813 ACLED conflict events (2018–2024). Two features of the data shape every comparison: UNESCO's verification starts with the full-scale invasion, so damage before 2022 is not recorded; and stolen objects are highly concentrated, with 1,233 coming from a single museum.

1. Time: do looting and destruction occur in the same periods?

Looting predates the full-scale invasion. 507 objects are recorded as stolen between 2014 and 2021, mostly from archaeological sites in occupied Crimea. The 2022 peak is a single event: of the 1,263 objects recorded in 2022, 1,184 come from the Kherson Art Museum, removed during the Russian withdrawal from the city. Excluding that museum, the yearly number of recorded thefts stays in the same range before and after 2022.

Damage records start in 2022, because UNESCO's monitoring starts with the invasion: 183 sites first reported in 2022, 53 in 2023 and 5 in 2024. With yearly data only, it is not possible to place looting inside or outside specific phases of combat; what the data show is that recorded looting has a longer time span than recorded destruction.

2. Space: do they affect the same regions?

Damaged sites are spread over 14 regions exposed to combat and long-range strikes: Donetsk (89), Kharkiv (55), Odesa (49), Kyiv (40), Luhansk (34) and Chernihiv (22) above all. Stolen objects come from only four regions: Kherson (1,300), Crimea (906), Donetsk (44, Mariupol) and Zaporizhzhia (37).

Crimea has no UNESCO damage records, which reflects the absence of fighting and of access for verification. Kherson is the only region with both many stolen objects and recorded damage (16 sites), consistent with a city first occupied and then, from November 2022, on the front line. Damage follows the regions exposed to combat and strikes; recorded looting is confined to territories under Russian control.

3. Context of violence: do they occur near the same kinds of conflict events?

Both datasets are compared with the same ACLED events (four event types, Ukraine, since 2022). For each damaged site and each place of incident of objects stolen from 2022 onward, we count the conflict events within 10 km and compare their composition with that of all ACLED events in Ukraine. Proximity is not causation, and only 12 looting locations are available.

Both phenomena occur in areas of intense conflict: the median damaged site has 512 ACLED events within 10 km, the median looting location 97. The mix of nearby events is similar: compared with the national average, both have fewer explosions and remote violence and more strategic developments. The "occupation and coercion" context (strategic developments and violence against civilians) is 15% of events near damaged sites and 14% near looting locations, against 7% nationally.

The type of conflict events recorded nearby therefore does not clearly distinguish destruction from looting. The difference between the two lies in where and when they are recorded, not in a different type of surrounding violence. ACLED's actor categories follow its own coding conventions (in Ukraine, foreign forces, including Russian forces, generally fall under "External/Other forces") and are not used to identify perpetrators.

4. What is damaged

UNESCO data show that damaged sites are mostly religious buildings (129), followed by historic buildings, museums, theatres and cultural institutions, and monuments: high-visibility heritage that anchors local identity and public memory.

Where the construction date is known (166 of 351 sites), damaged buildings date mostly from the Russian Empire, the Soviet period and independent Ukraine. This largely reflects the composition of Ukraine's urban built heritage; without a baseline of all heritage buildings, the data show which layers are affected, not whether any is affected disproportionately.

Findings
  1. Time. Recorded looting spans 2014–2025 and predates the full-scale invasion; damage to sites is recorded from 2022, when UNESCO's monitoring starts.
  2. Space. Damaged sites span 14 regions exposed to combat and strikes; stolen objects come from four regions, almost all incidents in territories under Russian occupation.
  3. Context of violence. Damaged sites and looting locations both lie in areas of intense conflict activity, with a similar mix of nearby ACLED events.
  4. A dual logic. Destruction follows the geography of combat, looting the geography of control: the two are spatially and temporally distinct, while the conflict events around them are similar. This does not prove deliberate targeting, but it complicates explanations that treat all cultural loss as undifferentiated collateral damage.

Research question 4
The limits of international protection

Does international recognition correspond to physical safety, and do institutional models of risk match documented theft?

We compare the 24 Ukrainian sites on UNESCO's lists (8 World Heritage properties and 16 Tentative List sites) with 211 damaged cultural sites recorded by Linked4Resilience, and the categories of the ICOM Emergency Red List for Ukraine with the registry of stolen objects.

1. The visibility gap: listed sites vs damaged sites

UNESCO lists are selective by design: they result from long nomination and evaluation processes. Damage reports cover a much broader set of cultural entities, most of which are not on any UNESCO list. Tentative List sites are candidates: they are not inscribed and do not carry World Heritage status.

In the waffle chart each square is one site: 24 yellow squares for UNESCO-listed sites and 211 blue squares for damaged sites. A blue square is not on a UNESCO list; this does not mean it is unprotected, since many damaged buildings are protected at national level, and some lie inside the boundaries of a listed historic centre such as Odesa. Use the filter to highlight one type of damaged site.

2. How close did damage come to listed sites?

Comparing Wikidata identifiers yields no match between listed and damaged sites. This is expected: UNESCO lists describe whole properties and ensembles (e.g. the Historic Centre of Odesa), while damage reports describe single buildings. We therefore measure geographic proximity: for each listed site in Ukraine with coordinates (16 sites), the geodesic distance from its reference coordinate (Wikidata) to the nearest damaged site.

Distances run from one point per site, not from the property boundary, so they are indicative; a point is a damaged site, not an attack; and occupied Crimea has no damage records, so the absence of nearby damage there reflects absence of data, not safety.

Five of the 16 listed sites have at least one damaged site within 1 km: the Historic Centre of Odesa (World Heritage, inscribed in January 2023 directly on the List of World Heritage in Danger; nearest damage 229 m, 19 damaged sites within 1 km) and Kyiv's Saint-Sophia Cathedral and Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra (World Heritage, 792 m), and three Tentative List sites: the historic centre of Chernihiv (80 m), Derzhprom in Kharkiv (609 m) and the Mykolaiv Astronomical Observatory (852 m). Most listed sites, including L'viv, are far from recorded damage.

3. Focus: the historic centre of Chernihiv

Chernihiv is the listed site with the closest recorded damage. It is on Ukraine's Tentative List (submitted in 1989): a candidate for World Heritage status, not an inscribed property. Ten damaged cultural sites lie within 1 km of its reference coordinate; the nearest, about 80 m away, is the Chernihiv Regional Taras Shevchenko Theatre, first reported damaged on 19 August 2023.

In the target view, the site's reference coordinate is at the centre and each dot is a damaged site placed at its real distance (the angle is only for readability). Recognition brings visibility, but not physical distance from war: exposure depends on where combat and long-range strikes occur, not on listing.

4. Beyond destruction: looting at a World Heritage site in occupied Crimea

The proximity analysis says nothing about occupied Crimea, where no damage is recorded. The registry of stolen objects documents a different form of harm at the World Heritage property Ancient City of Tauric Chersonese and its Chora (Sevastopol): 319 objects from the area of the site, 307 of them recorded as "appropriated during illegal excavations" and others as removed under the cover of exhibitions in Russia.

Here the gap between recognition and protection takes the form of appropriation rather than destruction, consistent with the pattern of looting in occupied territories observed in RQ3. The registry is maintained by a party to the conflict, and the year of incident is missing for most of these objects.

5. ICOM Red List vs reported stolen objects

The ICOM Emergency Red List for Ukraine is a typological guide for customs and law enforcement: it presents 53 example objects of categories considered at risk. It is not an inventory, and each example weighs about 2 percentage points. To compare it with the 2,287 stolen objects, both sources are mapped onto the same categories with the same rules, and the stolen objects are shown with and without the Kherson Art Museum.

Archaeology is the largest Red List category (30%) and, excluding the Kherson Art Museum, accounts for 84% of reported thefts: here risk model and documented theft align. Paintings (38% of stolen objects vs 13% in the Red List) and graphic works (16%) are driven almost entirely by the Kherson museum. Books and manuscripts, religious objects and applied arts make up 43% of the Red List but less than 2% of reported thefts, which may reflect looting patterns as well as what is documented. These differences describe documented cases, not the motives of looters.

Findings
  1. Visibility. International lists cover 24 sites; recorded damage concerns 211 identified sites, almost all outside those lists.
  2. Proximity. Five of 16 georeferenced listed sites have recorded damage within 1 km, including the World Heritage historic centre of Odesa (19 damaged sites within 1 km).
  3. Appropriation. In occupied Crimea, the World Heritage site of Tauric Chersonese is affected by removal of objects through excavation rather than by recorded destruction.
  4. Risk models. The Red List anticipates archaeological theft well, other categories less.
  5. Overall. Listing brings visibility, but not distance from war; institutional frameworks only partially capture how heritage is harmed.

07
/ Conclusions

Taken together, the four investigations suggest that cultural harm in Ukraine should not be approached as a single category.

Historical displacement (RQ1). The Hermitage inventory documents a long, centralised flow of Ukrainian archaeological material to St Petersburg: 14,508 objects, most of them acquired in the Soviet period, with acquisitions continuing after 1991. Many transfers were legal at the time: provenance, not legality alone, is the key issue.

Contemporary looting (RQ2). Recorded thefts start in 2014 in occupied Crimea and peak in 2022 with the removal of the Kherson Art Museum collection. Losses are concentrated in a few institutions and archaeological sites, almost all in territories under Russian occupation.

Destruction vs. looting (RQ3). Damaged sites are spread across the regions exposed to combat and long-range strikes; recorded looting is confined to occupied territories and has a longer history. The two are spatially and temporally distinct, while the conflict events recorded around them are similar: a dual logic in which destruction follows the geography of combat and looting the geography of control.

Limits of protection (RQ4). International listing brings visibility but not distance from war, and in occupied Crimea a World Heritage site is affected by appropriation rather than destruction. Risk typologies anticipate some categories of loss better than others.

What the data can and cannot show. The datasets are produced by institutions with different mandates, including a party to the conflict, and they record documented cases, not all losses. Patterns in time and space do not prove intent. What the data make visible is that destruction, looting, historical displacement and institutional appropriation operate through different mechanisms, which call for different documentation, protection, provenance and restitution strategies.

Despite international treaties prohibiting the destruction and looting of cultural heritage, notably the 1954 Hague Convention, governance mechanisms remain limited: institutions such as UNESCO document and monitor, but lack judicial power, and no enforceable international protocol regulates restitution during active conflicts. What does protection mean, if it cannot prevent destruction?

Some scholars and Ukrainian institutions describe these practices as cultural genocide. Establishing it would require evidence of intent, which patterns in data cannot provide on their own; this project does not make that claim.

Where does cultural harm begin: with military destruction, or with longer-term processes of displacement and appropriation?
If destruction removes cultural spaces while looting and displacement remove objects from their communities of origin, their convergence raises questions about cultural continuity, memory and identity. Rather than offering a definitive answer, this project proposes an evidence-based framework to investigate them critically, transparently and reproducibly.
